# Episode 1: Saiful Malook. Research dossier

**Status labels:** **[Verified]** = confirmed in a reliable source · **[Tradition]** = what people tell, attribute it ("کہا جاتا ہے") · **[Unverified]** = don't state as fact

---

## 1. The discovery that makes this episode: legend and real flood
- **[Verified] 1907 British record of the local legend.** *Gazetteer of the Hazara District, 1907* (ed. H. D. Watson), pp. 205–206 (archive.org, item in.ernet.dli.2015.31463). It calls the lake **"Safr Maluk Sar"**, "an enchanted lake", and records the story as Kaghan locals told it:
  - **The prince:** a **Prince of Delhi** sees a fairy of wondrous beauty in a dream. Astrologers send him to a glen in Kaghan above Naran for **12 years of religious devotion**.
  - **The fairies:** **"Badal Jamal, the Queen of the Fairies"** comes down with **360 attendants** to bathe. He snatches her clothes and won't return them until she promises to marry him.
  - **The flood:** a **powerful demon** who also loved her **bursts the embankment that dammed the lake**, and a mighty flood sweeps the valley. **Naran, "then a big city"**, is destroyed. The lake shifts to the centre of the valley "and was confined within narrower limits".
  - **The ending:** the couple escape to a hill, then go back to Delhi.
  - **Last line (quote it on screen):** *"Of the fairies some say that they have deserted the place, others aver that still of nights they come to dance their revels on the grass and bathe themselves in the stream, and then woe is it to the mortal who encounters them!"*
  - **On the name:** the narrator explained it as *safr* ("travel") + *mulk*, "the much-travelled one". The gazetteer adds that some say "Saif ul Maluk", "the sword of the chiefs".
  - **Lake size recorded then:** about half a mile by 500 yards; **10,718 ft** high.
  - The same gazetteer records that Akbar's blind daughter regained her sight bathing in nearby **Lulusar** lake. That's a possible future episode.
- **[Verified] The 2005 night the lake overflowed.** Dawn, published **26 June 2005**, dateline "Mansehra, June 25":
  - Snowmelt made the lake overflow on a Friday night, sending "torrents of water to Naran Bazaar".
  - Residents "fled their homes in panic", warned by mosque loudspeakers.
  - Villages named: Naraah, Taranna, Hassa, Batsung.
  - **No damage or casualties.**
  - Context: about 16 ft of snow that winter, then an unprecedented heatwave.
  - Source: https://www.dawn.com/news/145184
- **The angle:** the legend's demon breaking the lake's dam and flooding Naran describes a **real hazard**. The lake is a **glacial lake held back by a moraine** (a natural dam of rock and debris left by a glacier), and in 2005 its water really did reach Naran.
  - Present it as a striking parallel. It is **not proof** that the legend remembers a real flood.

## 1b. A century later: the same legend in the UNESCO survey (about 2006–07)
**[Verified]** UNESCO Islamabad, *Mapping and Documentation of the Cultural Assets of Kaghan Valley, Mansehra*, final report by **Prof. (Dr.) Ihsan Ali**, Hazara University; project begun 15 Sept 2006, after the 8 Oct 2005 earthquake. Section 4.1.2, p. 36: "Badr-i-Jamal and Prince Saif-ul-Malook".
- **The prince:** a **"Mughal prince"** (in 1907, a "Prince of Delhi") sees a fairy in a dream and is told to go to Naran for **twelve years' devotion**.
- **The name:** the report repeats the folk derivation from **"Safr Maluk"**, "the travel of countries".
- **The fairy and the demon:** the queen of the fairies, **Badar Jamal**, comes to bathe in the lake; the prince takes her clothes; she agrees to marry him; **"the demon who loved her smashed the embankment of the lake and thus flooded Naran."**
- **New detail, not in 1907:** *"The lovers hid in an extant cave under a mound of graves in Naran and escaped to live happily ever after in Delhi."* The legend has been tied to a specific spot in Naran town.
- **Songs and recordings:** p. 76 says the lake's "charismatic myth… has been interesting subject of mysterious stories and songs among the inhabitants of the area". The team **recorded folklore on audio cassettes** to be kept in a Hazara University museum. Possible lead: ask Hazara University's Department of Cultural Heritage and Tourism Management whether a clip can be used.
- **The author's recommendation:** the lake "should be preserved and added in the world Heritage of UNESCO". That is **the author's view, not a nomination**. Don't say UNESCO nominated it.
- **The lake then:** about half a mile long, about 8 km from Naran, a narrow jeep road crossing glaciers, a 2-hour walk; **"There is no hotel and restaurant in the surrounding area."** Contrast this with today's tent villages and the 2021 study's warning about pollution from them.
- **Height discrepancy:** the report says 3,500 m, against about 3,224 m elsewhere. Use about 3,200 m and avoid precise claims.
- **Ansoo Jheel:** named for its shape "like a tearing eye", reached via Manoor Kali.
- **The script line:** a hundred years apart, British officials in 1907 and Pakistani researchers in 2007 heard the **same story from the same valley**: the same demon, the same broken dam, the same flooded Naran.

**Other Kaghan folklore in the same report, for future episodes:**
- **Lala Afghani and Sohni:** Lalazar, said to mean "sacrifice". An Afghan boy is killed for loving a local girl; the forest is named "Sohni".
- **Munshi and Darshi:** "Darshi ka Jangal". A British-era clerk of a timber company is killed for loving a local girl; the Gujri folk song **"Qanchi"** tells their story.
- **The Sikh treasure of Pehli Lari:** villagers dig for it, an earthquake stops them.
- **Ghazi Baba of Khanian:** hoof prints on a rock; a "sword mark" removed during road building.
- **The blind brothers of Kameel Gali:** a dream, a digging, an earthquake, blindness.
- **The Maryam Zamani stone,** Balakot (240 lb).
- From the 1907 gazetteer: **Lulusar**, where Akbar's blind daughter is said to have regained her sight.

## 2. How the story travelled: about 1,000 years and 4,000 km
| When | Where | Version | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Medieval, a later addition | Arab world | **One Thousand and One Nights**: Egypt's old king Asim ibn Safwan and his vizier have no sons; Prophet **Sulaiman (Solomon)** helps. The prince Sayf al-Muluk falls in love with **a portrait embroidered on a robe Solomon gave**, labelled Badi'at al-Jamal, daughter of the jinn king **Shahyal** (ruler of Babel and the Garden of Iram). Captivity, escape, marriage. | [Verified]: described as a later addition to the Nights; surviving manuscripts date to about the 17th century |
| 1625 copy | Persia / Persian prose | *Hikayat-i Sayf al-Muluk va Badi' al-Jamal*: Persian prose tale; a 1625 Persian copy is at the US Library of Congress | [Verified] that it exists. **Image rights: check the item page before use** |
| 1035 AH (about 1625–26) | **Golconda, Deccan** | **Mulla Ghawwasi**, *Saif-ul-Mulook o Badi-ul-Jamal*, a Deccani **Urdu** masnavi for Sultan Abdullah Qutb Shah, translated from Persian | [Verified] (Sufinama, Rekhta edition 1938) |
| 1669–70 | **Arakan court (Mrauk U, today Myanmar)** | **Syed Alaol**, *Saiful Muluk Badiujjamal*, in **Bengali**, likely from a Persian source | [Verified] (Banglapedia, Wikipedia, d'Hubert, OUP 2018) |
| **Ramadan 1279 AH (1863)** | **Khari Sharif, near Mirpur** | **Mian Muhammad Bakhsh**, *Safar-ul-Ishq* ("Journey of Love"), known as **Saif-ul-Malook**. Punjabi; the prince is from Egypt; a Sufi love allegory. Written at age 33. | [Verified] |
| By 1907 | **Kaghan valley** | **Local oral version**: the prince is now **from Delhi**, the fairy is **Badal Jamal**, the stage is **this lake**, plus the demon and the flood of Naran | [Verified] that it was recorded (Gazetteer 1907) |
| Today | Kaghan | Guides and locals tell the legend to tourists; the prince is from Egypt in some versions and Delhi in others | [Tradition] |

**The line for the script:** a story born in the courts of Egypt and Baghdad passed through Persian, Deccani Urdu, Bengali and Punjabi, and settled at a lake in Kaghan. Along the way the Egyptian prince became a Delhi prince.

**[Unverified]:** exactly when the lake took the name. Wikipedia says only that the story "might have given the name to" the lake. Don't give a date.

## 3. The legend's details and their variants
- **[Tradition] The 40-day vigil:** in some versions the prince prays for 40 days; in the 1907 version it's **12 years**. Say that the versions differ.
- **[Tradition] Safaid Deo (the white giant)** holds the fairy captive at **Koh-e-Qaf**. This links to Episode 8.
- **[Tradition] Ansoo Jheel** formed from the giant's tears when he found the fairy gone. The 1907 version has no Ansoo; this is a newer layer.
- **[Tradition] The full moon:** the couple or the fairies come out on the 14th of every lunar month; some locals tell tourists to stay away then; some say they hear mysterious sounds; "glowing orbs" are said to cross the lake. Daily Times, Medium travel piece, folkloristan.
- **[Tradition] A cave by the shore** where the couple live. Versions disagree between the lake and the cave.

## 4. The real place: science and records
- **[Verified] Glacial lake,** dammed by glacial moraines. About **3,224 m** today; the 1907 figure of 10,718 ft is about 3,267 m (older surveying).
- **[Verified, secondary] Depth:** maximum about **34 m (113 ft)**; area about 2.75 km². **Myth to bust:** locals say it is bottomless. Source: Wikipedia, citing measurements; the original survey wasn't found, so say "according to measurements reported".
- **[Verified] Malika Parbat** ("Queen of the Mountains"): about **5,290 m**, the highest peak in Kaghan, about 6 km south of the lake. First climbed in **1920**.
  - **[Unverified]** that it's named after the fairy queen. Don't claim it.
- **[Verified] Ansoo Lake:** teardrop-shaped. **Said to** have been discovered in **1993** by Pakistan Air Force pilots flying low over Kaghan (Dawn 1574444; Wikipedia uses "said to"). Altitude estimates vary: about 4,126–4,245 m.
- **[Verified] Frozen in winter:** the road is closed about 5–6 months (November to April/May). Reopening work in 2026 began on 24 April after more than 5 months, with 6 glaciers on the 11 km road from Naran (APP / Tribune, April 2026).
- **[Verified] Water quality:** Taneez, Jamil & Ramzan, *Water, Air & Soil Pollution* (2021), samples from April and June 2019. The lake is "clean from chemical contamination", but **elevated BOD indicates the degradation process has started**. Pressures named: toilets, tent villages, plastic waste (Dawn).
- **[Tradition / weak source]** The lake's volume shrinking and colour changing (a poorly sourced Tribune item). Don't use.

## 5. Recent events: the "demon" in modern form
- **26 June 2005:** the lake overflowed into Naran Bazaar (see section 1).
- **26 March 2025:** a **glacier collapsed on the Lake Saiful Malook road in Naran**. The Kaghan Development Authority reported 6 houses, 3 hotels and 2 turbines affected; vehicle access cut; 3–4 ft of snow. No casualties reported. (Tribune 2536417)
- **25–26 July 2025:** a cloudburst and landslide blocked the lake road. **500+ tourists and 117 jeeps stranded**, all relocated safely. (Tribune 2558020; ProPakistani)
- **24–25 August 2025:** another landslide. **About 200 tourists evacuated** from the lake to Naran; nearby, in Kandia valley, families moved over a glacial-lake flood threat. (Dawn 1936932)
- **2025–26:** the Met Office installed early-warning stations at glacial lakes in Kandia. Its July 2026 advisory warns that expanding glacial lakes can destabilise ice or **moraine dams**. (Dawn 1939212; Tribune 2617686)
  - **Don't say** Saiful Malook itself is officially listed as a flood-burst risk. No source says so.

## 6. Story spine (for the script)
1. **Cold open (0:00–0:30):** night, June 2005, Naran. Mosque loudspeakers: leave your homes, the lake is overflowing. Then: a hundred years earlier, a British record wrote down a story the people of this valley already told, about a demon who broke this lake and drowned Naran.
2. **The legend, 1907 local version:** Delhi prince, the dream, 12 years, Badal Jamal and 360 fairies, the stolen clothes, the jealous demon, the dam, the flood. Then 2007: Pakistani researchers record the same story, now with a Mughal prince and a cave under a mound of graves in Naran where the lovers hid. **Re-hook at about 2:00:** this prince wasn't born in Kaghan. His story walked here over a thousand years.
3. **The journey:** Arabian Nights (Egypt, Sulaiman's robe) → Persian → Golconda 1625 (Ghawwasi, Urdu) → Arakan 1669 (Alaol, Bengali) → Khari Sharif 1863 (Mian Muhammad Bakhsh). The Egyptian prince becomes a Delhi prince in Kaghan.
4. **Re-hook at about 5:00:** newer layers. Ansoo Jheel (the giant's tear, "found" from the air in 1993) and the full-moon belief.
5. **The suraagh, science:**
   - A glacial lake, 34 m deep, not bottomless.
   - The real dam is a moraine.
   - 2005 overflow; 2025 glacier collapse, stranded tourists and flood alerts.
6. **Today:** frozen half the year; hundreds of thousands of visitors; the 2021 study shows the first signs of degradation.
7. **Ending:** the 1907 line, "some say the fairies have deserted the place…". Perhaps the fairies didn't leave because of a demon. **Comment question:** کیا آپ رات کو اِس جھیل کے کنارے رُکتے؟

## 8. Things to avoid saying
- That Malika Parbat is named after the fairy.
- A date for when the lake got its name.
- That Saiful Malook is an official flood-burst risk lake.
- The "Muhammad Tahir discovered Ansoo" claim.
- "Bottomless".
- Any haunting claim stated as fact.
