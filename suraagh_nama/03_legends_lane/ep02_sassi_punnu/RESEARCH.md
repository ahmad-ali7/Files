# Ep 2 — Sassi-Punnu — Research

**Standard:** same as Ep 1. Facts cross-checked across sources; **fact / evidence / tradition / speculation** kept separate.
Legend is the story; the episode's spine is the **real place + real evidence**. Never fabricate; disclose AI art.

## The thesis (the "Suraagh" / clue)
Everyone knows Sassi-Punnu as a desert love tragedy. What nobody tells: **the city it begins in, Bhambore, is
real — a rich port that genuinely died when the sea left it.** The legend of a girl the desert swallowed is the
folk memory of a *city* the desert swallowed. And a second clue: we aren't even sure Bhambore *is* Sassi's city —
it may be **Debal**, the port Muhammad bin Qasim stormed in 711. So we chase two trails: the lovers', and the city's.

## The legend (tradition — tell as the dastan)
- Sassui: born to the **Raja of Bhambore** (a Brahman ruler); a premonition said she'd bring dishonour, so she was
  set adrift on the **Indus** as an infant, and found and raised by a **washerman (dhobi)** of Bhambore.
- She grows famously beautiful; falls in love with **Punnu (Punhun)**, son of the **Baloch ruler of Kech (Makran)**.
- Punnu's family disapprove; his brothers get him drunk and carry him back to Kech by camel in the night.
- Sassui wakes to an empty city, runs **barefoot into the desert** after him. Lost and dying of thirst, she calls
  his name; the **earth/mountain opens and takes her**. A **shepherd** witnesses / tends her grave.
- Punnu, returning, learns from the shepherd, prays the same prayer; the earth takes him too. **Buried together.**
- Alleged joint grave: **Lasbela district**, Balochistan, in the **Hub / Pub range**, ~45 mi (≈65 km) west of
  Karachi. Heritage lists also note a "Sassi's spring." Punnu's fort is shown near **Turbat (Kech)**.
  *(Variants differ on Punnu's death — some say he's stabbed; most say the earth takes him. Treat as tradition.)*

## The real place & evidence (the spine)
- **Bhambore / Banbhore:** a genuine archaeological site on **Gharo Creek**, near Karachi/Thatta, Sindh.
- **Excavations (fact):** early soundings 1928 (R.C. Majumdar) and ~1951 (Leslie Alcock); the **major campaign
  1958–1965 under F.A. Khan** (Sindh Dept. of Archaeology); later **Pakistani–French–Italian** joint work to ~2015/2017.
- **Three periods (fact):** Scytho-Parthian (1st c. BC–2nd c. AD) · Hindu-Buddhist (2nd–8th c. AD) · early Islamic
  (8th–13th c. AD). A huge **ivory workshop** — reportedly ~**6,675 ivory pieces** — shows a wealthy trading port.
- **Why it died (evidence, consistent across sources incl. UNESCO tentative list):** the **Indus shifted course**
  and **Gharo Creek silted up** by the **13th century**, cutting the port off from the sea; trade moved away and the
  city was abandoned. *(A popular article claims earthquakes ~AD 280 / later — weak, single-source; treat cautiously.)*
- **Debal question (debated):** many scholars identify Bhambore with **Debal**, the port where **Muhammad bin Qasim
  defeated Raja Dahir (711–712)** — but the identification is **not settled / contested**. Good for the mystery, not to be stated as fact.

## The verse spine (like Ep 1's Mian Muhammad Bakhsh)
- **Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai (1689–1752)** devotes **Sur Sassui** in the **Shah Jo Risalo** (30 Surs) to this story.
- Mystical reading: Sassui's walk into the desert = the **soul's longing** for the divine; separation → reunion.
- **Decision (Ahmad):** we do **not** show an on-screen Sindhi verse. Instead we **honour Bhittai in Urdu** — his
  greatness, that he gave this desert love its immortal voice in *Sur Sassui*, and his status as the poet of
  longing and of Sindh. No exact-wording verification needed.

## Fact vs tradition (on-screen honesty)
- **Fact:** Bhambore exists, was a rich port, declined in the 13th c. as the Indus/creek changed; excavation history above.
- **Tradition:** that Bhambore is *Sassi's* city; the grave in Lasbela; the earth swallowing the lovers.
- **Debated:** Bhambore = Debal.
- We present tradition *as* tradition ("داستان کہتی ہے…") and keep the proven facts separate — the brand's whole edge.

## How the story travelled & got popular (the Ep-1 "travelling tale" beat) — DATED
Unlike Saiful Malook, which travelled *into* Pakistan (Alf Laila → Persian → Deccan → Bengali → Punjabi),
**Sassi-Punnu was born here** — in the Sindh–Makran desert — and spread *outward*. The written/sung chain, dated:

| Date | Teller | Lang. | What (source) |
|---|---|---|---|
| set ~14th–16th c. | — | — | Traditionally placed in **late-medieval Sindh (Samma era, 1351–1524, capital Thatta)**; characters: the "Raja of Bhambore" and "Jam Aali, Baloch ruler of Kech". *(Setting is traditional, not documented.)* |
| **c. 1463–1551** | **Qazi Qadan** | Sindhi | **The earliest known written mention** — and he is the **father of Sindhi poetry**. ⇒ the written tale is ~**500 years old**, and starts with the very first Sindhi poet. |
| **c. 1536–1623** | **Shah Abdul Karim of Bulri** | Sindhi | Mentions it in his *Karim Jo Risalo*. He is **Bhittai's own great-great-grandfather** — the tale runs in Bhittai's family line of poets. |
| **1689–1752** | **Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai** | Sindhi | *Sur Sassui* in the *Shah Jo Risalo*; Sassui becomes one of the **Seven Queens**; the Sufi-allegory version that made it immortal. |
| **18th c.** | **Hashim Shah** | Punjabi | *Qissa Sassi Punnun*; his Sassi dies crossing the desert. |
| **19th c.** | **Fazal Shah Sayyad** (Lahore) | Punjabi | The **most popular Punjabi rendition**. |
| modern | Ram Panjwani (Sindhi play); films 1954/1983/2004 | — | Kept alive on stage and screen. |

**Why it got popular (4 reasons):** (1) rooted in **real named places** (Bhambore, Kech) so it felt true; (2) **Bhittai made it
sacred** — one of the Seven Queens, her desert walk read as the **soul's longing for God**; (3) it is **continuously sung**
at Sufi shrines, above all at **Bhit Shah during Bhittai's urs**; (4) it crossed **Sindhi, Punjabi, Balochi, Siraiki** —
four peoples each claim it. In Sindh it's one of the **seven** great romances; in Punjab, one of the **four**.
*(Poet life-dates are their established dates, attached by us; the Wikipedia article names the tellers but not the years.
Qazi Qadan as "earliest mention" is per the English Wikipedia Origins section — supersedes the earlier garbled "Qadir Shah".)*

## ✅ Primary-source reading (ACTUALLY READ — 2026-11-27)
Read verbatim: **Cousens 1929, the Bhambor chapter (pp. 80–81)** via the archive.org full text, and the
LiveHistoryIndia Debal article in full. (The paid reports — F.A. Khan, Parthica — and Cousens pp. 5–30/46–63
are **not yet read**; claims from them stay flagged.)

**What Cousens 1929 actually says (our on-screen document) — big upgrades:**
- **The record names the place after Sassi.** Cousens: the ruins have "been connected with the loves of Sassui
  and Punhun, a favourite story still told in the homes of Sind," and **the site "is thus also called
  Sassui-jo-Takar"** (Sassi's Hill/Mound). ⇒ a *real 1929 archaeological book* ties the legend to the place and
  even calls the hill by her name. This is the Ep-2 equivalent of Ep 1's Gazetteer validating the fairies. **Centrepiece.**
- **Cousens REJECTED Bhambor = Debal** — "too insignificant a site to have been that of the city which for some
  time defied the forces of the Arab invaders"; he thought it only "an outpost guarding the creek, or a small port."
  A footnote: the *Tuhfatu-l-Kiram* "mentions both Debal and Bhambor as two different places." ⇒ the Debal question
  is a **century-old live dispute**, not a modern footnote: 1920s diggers said Debal; Cousens (1929) said no.
- **A one-night destruction legend.** Via Elliot, Cousens records the town was destroyed "in a single night, to the
  divine wrath which its ruler's sins drew down upon it," its fall "mixed up with the story of King Dalu Rai"
  (presented as legend). ⇒ a second eerie layer: a sinful king, a city gone overnight — folk memory of a real death.
- **The river left it (primary-sourced).** "When the creek became disconnected with the Indus, the place was most
  likely then abandoned and fell into ruin"; the sea "was not so far from it as now"; Cunningham thought it deserted
  "about A.D. 1250," once the Indus's main western branch flowed past it.
- **Quotable physical detail:** site "600 by 400 yards"; outer wall "8 to 10 feet wide"; "33 miles east of Karachi";
  "Nothing now remains but the foundations of walls"; corroded copper coins, shells in abundance, glazed pottery,
  glass, clay coin moulds.
- **Caveats (OCR / unread):** "A.D. 1250" and "Bhambo Raja" are rough OCR — verify exact wording before on-screen quote.
  A possible pp.14–30 "Bhanbhorai founded Bhambor" + Sassi footnote was **not** verified — do not assert it yet.

**What the PARTHICA 18 (2016) peer-reviewed report adds (Pak-Italian mission, READ — cite only, © do NOT show/commit):**
- Hard, authoritative figures: Banbhore sits at the Indus delta mouth on the Gharo creek, **~30 km from today's
  shoreline** (the sea really pulled back); citadel ringed by a wall with **47 circular towers + 8 rectangular
  bastions**, an **artificial sweet-water lake** to the NE, harbour structures, workshops, warehouses; the whole
  **~65 hectares**. A N–S **"Partition Wall"** splits the citadel in two (the temple/mosque halves).
- **The Debal dispute, with the actual camps named** (via Kervran's survey): those who **denied** Banbhore = Daybul
  (Debal) **or** the Greek **Barbarikon** of the *Periplus* — **H.M. Elliot, H. Haig, H.G. Raverty, H.T. Lambrick,
  Henry Cousens**. Those who **argued it IS Daybul** — **L. Alcock, M. Rafique Mughal, F.A. Khan** (the systematic
  1958–65 digs), backed by the great historian **N.A. Baloch**. The debate opened with Cousens' 1929 visit and
  Majumdar's 1930 trenches and has never closed.
- ⇒ **The city may have THREE names and we're sure of none:** **Barbarikon** (Greek/Roman delta port, Periplus),
  **Debal/Daybul** (the Arab port Qasim stormed, 711), or **Bhambor / "Sassui-jo-Takar"** (the legend). One buried
  city, three identities — a superb second mystery.
- F.A. Khan's 1958–65 excavation was **"left only partially studied and published"** — i.e. the city still guards
  its secrets. Good narration beat.

**What the Debal article adds (modern view):**
- Mosque with **Kufic inscriptions dated 727 CE and 906 CE**; if 727 is the build date, among the earliest mosques
  in the subcontinent (16 yrs after Qasim). Mosque plan 34×35 m. A wall split the city **east/west — a Shiva temple
  in the west, the mosque in the east** (a layered pagan→Muslim city). Trade goods from Syria, Iran, Iraq, China.
  The Debal story rests on the 8th-c. **Chachnama** (attack triggered by piracy off Debal).
- **Ivory figure correction:** this article says **~40 kg of worked ivory** (earlier "~6,675 pieces" is a separate
  popular claim). Present as "thousands of ivory pieces / about 40 kg — reported," not as a hard count.
