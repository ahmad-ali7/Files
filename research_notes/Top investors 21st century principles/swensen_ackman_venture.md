# Swensen (Yale), Ackman (Pershing Square), and the Top Venture/Private-Market Records, 2000–2026

Research cutoff: 2 November 2026. "Verified" = from a primary source (Yale Investments Office or Yale News, Pershing Square filings and annual reports, SEC filings, company releases) or several consistent major outlets. "Estimated/unverified" is flagged inline.

## Q1. Swensen and the Yale model: returns, allocation, the advice to individuals, and whether the model still works after 2021

### Takeaway
Under Swensen (CIO 1985–2021) Yale returned 13.7% a year over his full tenure and 11.3% a year over the 20 years to June 2021. That beat domestic stocks (9.1%), bonds (4.6%) and the average endowment (7.7%). After his death the record is mixed. FY2023 (1.8%), FY2024 (5.7%) and FY2025 (11.1%) all trailed simple public-market portfolios. Yale also sold about $2.5–6bn of private-equity stakes on the secondary market for the first time in 2025. Then FY2026 rebounded to 26.3%, which lifted the 10-year return to 11.7% a year, 2.7 points a year above a 70/30 portfolio. For individuals, Swensen's own 2005 book *Unconventional Success* says not to copy the Yale model. He told them to hold low-cost index funds instead, preferably from not-for-profit providers such as Vanguard and TIAA-CREF.

**Swensen's advice to individuals: *Unconventional Success* (2005)**
- Swensen tells individual investors to own broadly diversified index funds that track the market rather than try to beat it. He wants them bought through low-fee, not-for-profit fund companies such as Vanguard and TIAA-CREF, because for-profit fund managers have a conflict of interest. — [NPR, 2006](https://www.npr.org/2006/10/05/6204218/david-swensens-advice-for-the-individual-investor); [KUNC/NPR](https://www.kunc.org/npr-news/2006-10-05/david-swensens-advice-for-the-individual-investor)
- NPR summarises his reasoning: "since at least 99 percent of mutual funds aren't going to beat the overall market", individuals should use non-profit index funds. No source was found for a "95% fail" figure. — [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2006/10/05/6204218/david-swensens-advice-for-the-individual-investor)
- A Motley Fool review notes the advice is "hardly unconventional", only unusually strongly argued. — [Motley Fool, 2005](https://www.fool.com/investing/mutual-funds/2005/10/14/quotunconventional-successquot-conventional-foolis.aspx)
- His model portfolio for individuals, widely reproduced in commentary, is roughly 30% US stocks, 15% developed international, 5% emerging markets, 20% REITs, 15% Treasuries and 15% TIPS. This comes from secondary commentary and was not checked against the book. — [StockCharts](https://stockcharts.com:443/articles/muscular-investing/2019/07/unconventional-success-portfolio.html)
- His institutional book, *Pioneering Portfolio Management* (2000; revised 2009), describes the endowment approach: equity-oriented, diversified, heavy in illiquid alternatives, and relying on top-quartile external managers. — [Yale News coda](https://news.yale.edu/2021/10/22/david-swensens-coda) (general context)

### Inferences
- Rough arithmetic of my own, not a reported figure: a 60/40 blend of Yale's quoted 20-year stock (9.1%) and bond (4.6%) returns gives about 7.3% a year to 2021. Yale's 11.3% beat that by about 4 points a year, consistent with the third-party "4.0% over tenure" claim.
- The "Yale model is broken" story peaked in 2023–2025, when private-heavy portfolios lagged a US equity bull market and distributions dried up. FY2026's 26.3% weakens it, but Yale still trailed Penn and Harvard, and the 2025 forced-liquidity sale showed what illiquidity costs in practice.
- The key point for the final report: the architect of the endowment model said publicly that individuals should not copy it. He believed the edge came from access to top-quartile managers, big staffs and a perpetual horizon, none of which retail investors have.

## Q2. Ackman and Pershing Square: returns since 2004, wins, losses, principles, and 2025–2026 developments

### Takeaway
From 1 January 2004 to 10 February 2026, Pershing Square's flagship strategy compounded at about 15.9% a year net, against 10.7% for the S&P 500 (2,506% cumulative against 851%). Almost all of the outperformance came in 2004–2010 and 2019–2020. Since PSH's own 2012 inception it has returned 12.9% a year net, behind the S&P 500's 14.9%. That gap reflects the 2015–2018 drawdown (Valeant, Herbalife) and the persistent discount to NAV. 2025 was strong: NAV up 20.9% against 17.9% for the S&P 500. In 2025–2026 Ackman took control of Howard Hughes as a "modern Berkshire" holding company. He then raised $5bn in a combined April 2026 IPO of Pershing Square USA (PSUS) and the manager, Pershing Square Inc. (PS). PSUS fell about 18% on day one and was about 21% below its IPO price by September 2026.

The share price rose 33.9% as the discount to NAV narrowed from 31.2% to 24.1%. — [PSH 2025 Annual Report letter via MarketScreener](https://www.marketscreener.com/news/pershing-square-letter-to-shareholders-in-the-2025-annual-report-ce7e5ddfd98ff620); [PSH 2025 Annual Report PDF](https://assets.pershingsquareholdings.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/18175039/Pershing-Square-Holdings-Ltd.-2025-Annual-Report.pdf)

**Stated principles**
- PSH's stated objective is to "maximize long-term compound annual growth in intrinsic value per share". It runs a concentrated portfolio of 8–12 core holdings, mostly liquid, large-cap North American companies. It seeks "high-quality growth businesses" with limited downside and "predictable, recurring cash flows", and uses "opportunistic hedging" to limit downside or capture asymmetric opportunities. — [PSH 2025 Annual Report](https://www.marketscreener.com/news/pershing-square-letter-to-shareholders-in-the-2025-annual-report-ce7e5ddfd98ff620)
- As of August 2026, 12 stocks made up 86% of the portfolio. The top holdings were Microsoft, Uber and Meta, followed by Brookfield, Amazon and Restaurant Brands. Ackman expects each holding to grow EPS at least 15% a year over 3–5 years, with half growing 20% or more. His stated goal is gross returns "in excess of 20% per annum over the long-term". — [Motley Fool, Sep 2026](https://www.fool.com/investing/2026/09/04/bill-ackman-says-pershing-square-has-achieved-a-20/)

He used the proceeds to buy stocks near the bottom, which drove 2020's 70.2% NAV return. The figures appear consistently across secondary sources and match Pershing's public statements at the time. One blog's "$200m profit" claim is unsupported. — [Wikipedia: Bill Ackman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Ackman); [Valens Research](https://www.valens-research.com/investor-essentials-daily/infamous-activist-short-seller-made-living-from-massive-covid-trade/); [WebProNews](https://www.webpronews.com/ackmans-2-6-billion-storm-bet-how-one-hedge-mastered-the-covid-chaos/)

- Howard Hughes Holdings, a GGP spin-off in 2010, became a long-term holding. In May 2025 Pershing agreed to buy 9m newly issued HHH shares at $100 each ($900m), a 48% premium to the $67.71 close. ### Inferences
- Concentrated activism produces lumpy returns. Two periods (2004–2010 and 2019–2020) account for the alpha, and one period (2015–2018) erased much of it. Investors in the 2012-launched PSH have done worse than the S&P 500 index fund that Swensen would have recommended.
- PSH's persistent 20–30% discount to NAV, and PSUS trading roughly 20% below its IPO price, show a further layer of drag for outside investors in closed-end vehicles beyond manager performance.
- The COVID hedge cost about 0.2% of a roughly $11–12bn portfolio, an asymmetric tail-hedge. It is the clearest example of the "opportunistic hedging" principle. (The portfolio size is my approximation and not sourced.)

## Q3. Venture and private-market records: Thiel/Facebook, Sequoia, the power law, return dispersion, SoftBank, and what individuals can replicate

### Takeaway
The century's biggest venture wins are extreme outliers. Thiel's $500k into Facebook (2004) became more than $1bn in 2012 sales, at least 2,000 times the cost. Sequoia's roughly $60m into WhatsApp was worth about $3bn at Facebook's 2014 $19bn acquisition, about 50 times. Son's $20m into Alibaba (2000) grew into a stake worth more than $100bn. The asset class as a whole is less impressive. Kauffman's 20-year review of about 100 VC funds found only 20 beat public markets by the 3%+ a year needed to justify illiquidity, with an average multiple of about 1.3x. Deal-level data show about 65% of VC investments return less than cost. SoftBank's Vision Fund is a mixed case: losses of about $14bn on WeWork and a record loss in 2022, offset by Arm and, in FY2025, a $45bn paper gain on OpenAI.

### Cited Findings
**Peter Thiel / Facebook**
- In August 2004 Thiel became Facebook's first outside investor, putting in $500,000 for a 10.2% stake. He sold 16.8m shares at the May 2012 IPO for $638m. When the lock-up ended in August 2012 he sold about 20.1m more shares at $19.27–$20.69 (about $396m), taking total proceeds past $1bn. Further sales came later, including in 2017. — [CNN Money, Aug 2012](https://money.cnn.com/2012/08/20/technology/facebook-peter-thiel/); [The Drum](https://www.thedrum.com/news/2012/08/22/paypal-co-founder-peter-thiel-one-first-sell-his-facebook-shares-raking-1bn); [CNBC 2017](https://www.cnbc.com/2017/11/22/peter-thiel-sells-majority-of-facebook-shares-but-2012-was-bigger.html)
- Thiel later acknowledged he sold too early and "misjudged big". — [Fortune, July 2025](https://www.fortune.com/2025/07/01/billionaire-paypal-palantir-cofounder-peter-thiel-first-outside-facebook-investor-sold-early-ipo-missed-billions); [Benzinga](https://www.benzinga.com/markets/tech/25/07/46509918/peter-thiel-once-touched-on-selling-facebook-stock-too-early-palantir-co-founder-says-misjudged-big-)
- Stake conflict: 10.2% in 2004, against about 2.5% at the 2012 IPO, a difference explained by dilution. — [CNBC 2017](https://www.cnbc.com/2017/11/22/peter-thiel-sells-majority-of-facebook-shares-but-2012-was-bigger.html)

**Sequoia / WhatsApp (and Google)**
- Sequoia invested about $60m across three rounds for a stake of roughly 18–20%. At Facebook's $19bn acquisition in February 2014 ($4bn cash, $12bn in Facebook stock, $3bn in restricted stock units), the stake was valued at about $3bn, an estimate rather than a realised figure. Other estimates run to about $3.2bn, or "$3bn to nearly $7bn" depending on the ownership assumed. **Venture return dispersion and the power law**
- Kauffman Foundation, May 2012, "We Have Met the Enemy… and He Is Us": a review of about 100 VC funds over about 20 years. Only 20 of 100 beat a public-market equivalent by more than the 3–5% a year premium Kauffman expected for illiquidity and risk. The portfolio returned about 1.3x invested capital against a roughly 2x target. The strongest excess returns came from small funds raised before 1996. Kauffman shifted toward smaller funds and public index exposure. — [Kauffman report page](https://kauffman.org/reports/we-have-met-the-enemy-and-he-is-us); [archived PDF](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20121218010420/http://www.kauffman.org/uploadedFiles/vc-enemy-is-us-report.pdf); [HuffPost (report authors)](https://www.huffpost.com/entry/venture-capital-myths_b_1533451); [ThinkAdvisor](https://www.thinkadvisor.com/2012/05/22/learning-the-hard-way/)

- Deal level: Correlation Ventures data on about 27,000 deals (2009–2018) show about 65% returned less than 1x and about 0.4% returned more than 50x. Horsley Bridge data show about 6% of deals generated about 60% of returns. ### Inferences
- The Kauffman counts probably do not conflict. "62 of 100 failed to beat public markets" (so 38 beat them) and "only 20 beat them by more than 3% a year" can both be true. I believe both statements appear in the original report, but the PDF was not fetched to confirm.
- Venture's headline records (Facebook at about 2,000x, WhatsApp at about 50x, Alibaba at about 5,000x on paper) are power-law outliers. They are mostly reached through early private access, board relationships or proprietary deal flow, which individuals cannot get. The median fund roughly matches or trails public equity after fees, illiquidity and the J-curve, which is the Kauffman conclusion.
- SoftBank shows the same power law at the scale of a whole firm. One or two bets (Alibaba then, OpenAI now) offset many large failures (WeWork, Katerra, Greensill, DiDi). Its net return is highly path-dependent and, with OpenAI, remains largely unrealised.
- What individuals can and cannot copy:
  - **Can:** Swensen's individual prescription of diversified, low-cost index funds with rebalancing. Ackman's principles of buying quality, predictable free-cash-flow businesses and holding a concentrated, long-horizon portfolio, though concentration carries the risk shown in 2015–2018. Exposure to listed vehicles (PSH, PSUS, SoftBank stock), with discount and governance risk.
  - **Cannot:** top-quartile manager access, early-stage deal flow, the illiquidity premium at institutional scale, activist board influence, or cheap OTC derivatives hedges like the 2020 CDS trade.
