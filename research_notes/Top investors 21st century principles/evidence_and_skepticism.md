# Evidence and Skepticism: What the Data Says About "Learn From Top Investors" Advice (2000–2026)

Research date: 2 November 2026. All statistics carry their as-of dates. Where a figure comes from a secondary source or search excerpt rather than the primary document, this is flagged.

## 1. SPIVA scorecards: how often do active funds beat their benchmarks, and does outperformance persist?

### Takeaway
The latest S&P SPIVA U.S. Year-End 2025 data (as of 31 Dec 2025) shows that about 90–95% of active U.S. equity funds trailed their benchmarks over 10–20 years, and roughly 97–98% did so on a risk-adjusted basis. Only about a third of funds even survived 20 years. Persistence scorecards find that top-quartile performance almost never lasts, which is the key empirical argument against copying whoever has the best recent record.

### Cited Findings
**SPIVA U.S. Year-End 2025 (data as of 31 Dec 2025; read from the full PDF)**
- 79% of active large-cap U.S. equity funds underperformed the S&P 500 in 2025, up from 65% in 2024. S&P calls it the fourth-worst year in the scorecard's 25-year history. The reason it gives is "unrelenting large-cap outperformance." — [SPIVA U.S. Year-End 2025 (S&P DJI)](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/spiva/article/spiva-us-year-end-2025/); [full PDF copy](https://www.ariesadvisors.com/files/spiva-us-year-end-2025.pdf)
- Report 1a, % underperforming on absolute return (1 / 3 / 5 / 10 / 15 / 20 years) — [SPIVA U.S. YE2025 PDF](https://www.ariesadvisors.com/files/spiva-us-year-end-2025.pdf):
  - All Domestic Funds vs S&P Composite 1500: 79.83 / 80.40 / 91.47 / **90.43 / 93.15 / 95.01**
  - All Large-Cap vs S&P 500: 78.78 / 66.84 / 88.96 / **85.59 / 89.93 / 92.89**
  - All Mid-Cap vs S&P MidCap 400: 55.41 / 62.93 / 72.32 / 81.14 / 84.49 / 89.74
  - All Small-Cap vs S&P SmallCap 600: 40.65 / 42.18 / 62.67 / 75.95 / 89.90 / 90.28
  - Large-Cap Growth vs S&P 500 Growth: 95.51 / 56.33 / 95.26 / 91.67 / 97.82 / **99.55**
  - Large-Cap Value vs S&P 500 Value: 41.30 / 80.51 / 83.03 / 88.45 / 93.29 / 87.01
- Report 1b, risk-adjusted (3 / 5 / 10 / 15 / 20 years): All Domestic 88.47 / 92.98 / 96.17 / 98.05 / 97.67. All Large-Cap 82.76 / 91.14 / 95.73 / 98.61 / 97.76. — [SPIVA U.S. YE2025 PDF](https://www.ariesadvisors.com/files/spiva-us-year-end-2025.pdf)
- **Survivorship (Report 2):** Over 20 years, only 36.53% of 2,447 domestic equity funds survived, and only 34.61% of 760 large-cap funds. Over 15 years the figures were 50.92% of all domestic funds and 50.55% of large-cap funds. Twenty-year style consistency was 47.28% for domestic funds and 57.37% for large-cap. In other words, most funds that exist at the start of a 20-year window are merged or liquidated, and more than half drift out of their style. — [SPIVA U.S. YE2025 PDF](https://www.ariesadvisors.com/files/spiva-us-year-end-2025.pdf)
- **International funds, 2025 one-year:** 63% of International funds, 76% of Global funds and 53% of Emerging Markets funds underperformed. — [SPIVA U.S. YE2025 PDF](https://www.ariesadvisors.com/files/spiva-us-year-end-2025.pdf)
- Report 6a, international, absolute return (10 / 15 / 20 years): Global Funds vs S&P World 93.41 / 95.63 / n.a. International vs S&P World ex-U.S. 89.78 / 92.70 / n.a. Emerging Markets vs S&P Emerging Plus 87.70 / 89.58 / 94.37. International Small-Cap 73.81 / 78.85 / 84.78. Risk-adjusted 15-year Global: 99.45%. — [SPIVA U.S. YE2025 PDF](https://www.ariesadvisors.com/files/spiva-us-year-end-2025.pdf)
- **Bonds, 2025:** 82% of General Investment-Grade and 76% of High Yield funds underperformed in 2025. High Yield underperformed at 86.61% over 10 years and 82.35% over 20 years (column order per the PDF table). — [SPIVA U.S. YE2025 PDF](https://www.ariesadvisors.com/files/spiva-us-year-end-2025.pdf)
- **Institutional:** 67% of institutional accounts and 75% of wrap accounts in large-cap underperformed the S&P 500 in 2025, measured gross of fees. — [SPIVA Institutional Scorecard YE2025](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/documents/spiva/spiva-institutional-scorecard-year-end-2025.pdf) (search excerpt)
- Methodology: SPIVA includes funds that were liquidated or merged, which removes survivorship bias, and it reports both equal-weighted and asset-weighted results. — [CXO Advisory summary of SPIVA YE2025, 15 Jul 2026](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/investing-expertise/performance-of-active-u-s-mutual-funds-and-etfs/)

**U.S. Persistence Scorecard Year-End 2025 (data as of 31 Dec 2025; figures from search excerpts of the official PDF, not read in full)**
- Of 173 large-cap funds in the top quartile at Dec 2023, 28.90% were still top quartile at Dec 2025. Of 521 domestic funds, 33.01% were. Mid-cap stood at 22.73%. — [U.S. Persistence Scorecard YE2025 PDF](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/documents/spiva/persistence-scorecard-year-end-2025.pdf)
- In every reported active domestic equity category except small-cap, no top-quartile fund from 2021 was still top quartile at the end of 2025. One secondary summary puts the share for all domestic equity funds at 0.46%. — [Persistence Scorecard YE2025](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/documents/spiva/persistence-scorecard-year-end-2025.pdf); [TKer summary](https://www.tker.co/p/spiva-persistence-2025-past-performance-no-guarantee)
- Top-half persistence is somewhat better: 49.4% of 338 large-cap top-half funds in 2023 were still in the top half at end-2025, against about 25% expected by chance. — [Advisor Perspectives, 8 May 2026](https://www.advisorperspectives.com/commentaries/2026/05/08/s-p-persistence-scorecard-reveals-universal-struggles-for-active-strategies) (secondary)
- Europe Persistence Scorecard: S&P found "no strong evidence for persistence in relative performance" in any category or time horizon examined. — [S&P Europe Persistence Scorecard](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/spiva/article/europe-persistence-scorecard/)
- Australia: 16.0% of 2023 top-quartile equity funds stayed top quartile in both 2024 and 2025. Bond funds did better, at 41.2%. — [Financial Standard on SPIVA Australia Persistence](https://www.financialstandard.com.au/news/aussie-active-equity-managers-lack-persistent-outperformance-spiva-179812516)

### Inferences
- Selecting a fund or manager because of a strong recent record (the "learn from or copy the winners" heuristic) is close to a coin flip at best. Past top-quartile status predicts very little about future top-quartile status.
- Survivorship bias is central. Two-thirds of the funds alive 20 years ago are gone, so any list of "great investors of 2000–2026" is drawn from survivors. The failures are invisible by construction.
- Underperformance is worse on a risk-adjusted basis (around 97–98% over 15–20 years), so the minority that "won" often did so by taking more risk.
- Category matters. Small-cap and value funds look better in single years when the benchmark is hard to beat for structural reasons, such as mega-cap dominance in 2023–2025. Over 20 years every category is still roughly 85–99% underperforming.

- I could not open the S&P web pages directly (HTTP 403). The U.S. figures come from a full PDF copy of the official scorecard hosted by an advisory firm (text-extracted). Persistence figures come from search excerpts of the official PDF plus secondary commentary. The 0.46% and 49.4% figures should be checked against the primary PDF.
- I did not retrieve SPIVA Europe, Japan or India long-horizon underperformance tables for year-end 2025.
- The 2026 mid-year SPIVA U.S. scorecard (data to 30 Jun 2026) may exist. One secondary source listed a 20-year large-cap figure of 92.61% labeled June 2026, but I could not verify it.

## 2. Academic evidence on skill, luck and persistence

### Takeaway
The academic consensus is that the average active fund delivers negative alpha net of fees, roughly equal to its costs. A small tail of managers may have genuine skill before fees, but it is hard to identify in advance, and the persistence that exists sits mainly at the bottom (bad funds stay bad). Buffett's record, the strongest "skill" case, is largely explained by systematic factors (cheap, safe, quality stocks) plus leverage. Stock-market wealth is extremely concentrated in a few stocks, so concentrated portfolios usually miss the winners. All of this argues for diversification and against emulating concentrated stock pickers.

### Cited Findings
**Fama & French (2010), "Luck versus Skill in the Cross-Section of Mutual Fund Returns," *Journal of Finance* 65(5): 1915–1947**
- The aggregate portfolio of active U.S. equity mutual funds is close to the market portfolio, and its net alpha is negative by roughly the amount of fund expenses. — [Fama & French 2010 (PDF)](https://mba.tuck.dartmouth.edu/bespeneckbo/default/AFA611-Eckbo%20web%20site/AFA611-S8C-FamaFrench-LuckvSkill-JF10.pdf); [RePEc record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:65:y:2010:i:5:p:1915-1947)
- Bootstrap simulations suggest few funds produce benchmark-adjusted expected returns large enough to cover their costs. Gross of fees, there is evidence of both inferior and superior true alpha in the extreme tails. The authors say true net alpha is "negative for most if not all active funds." — [Fama & French 2010 (PDF)](https://mba.tuck.dartmouth.edu/bespeneckbo/default/AFA611-Eckbo%20web%20site/AFA611-S8C-FamaFrench-LuckvSkill-JF10.pdf)
- Contested: Kosowski et al. (2006, JF) used a similar bootstrap and found more evidence of skill. Harvey & Liu (2022, JF) argue both methods have problems and propose adjustments, so the size of the skilled tail is still debated. — [Harvey & Liu, "Luck versus Skill" (Duke PDF)](https://people.duke.edu/~charvey/Research/Published_Papers/P151_Luck_versus_skill.pdf)

**Carhart (1997), "On Persistence in Mutual Fund Performance," *Journal of Finance* 52(1): 57–82**
- Common factors (market, size, value, one-year momentum) plus differences in expenses and transaction costs explain almost all of the predictability in mutual fund returns. The only significant persistence not explained is the strong underperformance of the worst-decile funds. Carhart concludes the results "do not support the existence of skilled or informed mutual fund portfolio managers." — [Carhart 1997, JF (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1997.tb03808.x) (summary from my knowledge of the paper's abstract; the page was not fetched in this session)

**Frazzini, Kabiller & Pedersen, "Buffett's Alpha," *Financial Analysts Journal* 74(4), 2018: 35–55**
- Berkshire Hathaway's Sharpe ratio was 0.79 (1976–2017 in the FAJ version), and it had significant alpha against traditional factors. That alpha becomes statistically insignificant once the Betting-Against-Beta (safe, low-beta stocks) and Quality-Minus-Junk factors are added. — [SSRN abstract 3197185](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3197185); [RePEc FAJ record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:ufajxx:v:74:y:2018:i:4:p:35-55); [CFA Institute FAJ page](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/en/research/financial-analysts-journal/2018/faj-v74-n4-3)
- The FAJ version estimates average leverage at about 1.7-to-1. The earlier 2013 NBER working paper (w19681) reported about 1.6-to-1 and a Sharpe ratio of 0.76 for 1976–2011. The leverage came mainly from insurance float, at below-T-bill funding cost, plus deferred taxes. — [NBER WP 19681 (RePEc)](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:nbr:nberwo:19681); [Validea summary](https://blog.validea.com/an-analysis-of-buffetts-alpha/)
- The authors conclude that Buffett's returns look less like "luck or magic" and more like a reward for leveraging cheap, safe, high-quality stocks. Berkshire's portfolio of public stocks did better than its wholly owned private companies, which suggests the returns come from stock selection rather than from his influence on management. — [Validea summary](https://blog.validea.com/an-analysis-of-buffetts-alpha/); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3197185)

**Bessembinder (2018), "Do Stocks Outperform Treasury Bills?" *Journal of Financial Economics* 129(3): 440–457**
- From 1926 to 2016, the best-performing ~4% of U.S. listed companies (1,092 of about 26,000 stocks, or 4.3%) account for all net wealth creation above one-month T-bills. The other stocks collectively matched T-bills. — [Bessembinder JFE paper (ASU PDF)](https://wpcarey.asu.edu/sites/default/files/2021-10/do-stocks-outperform-treasury-bills.pdf); [ASU news summary](https://news.wpcarey.asu.edu/20180917-do-stocks-outperform-treasury-bills); [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:129:y:2018:i:3:p:440-457)
- More than half of CRSP common stocks have negative lifetime returns. About 57.4% have lifetime buy-and-hold returns below one-month T-bills. An ASU summary also cites a figure of 38% "failed to beat" T-bills, which appears to be counted differently, so treat it with care. — [Bessembinder paper PDF](https://wpcarey.asu.edu/sites/default/files/2021-10/do-stocks-outperform-treasury-bills.pdf); [ASU news](https://news.wpcarey.asu.edu/20180917-do-stocks-outperform-treasury-bills)

**Factor evidence and the long value drawdown (AQR)**
- Israel, Laursen & Richardson (AQR), "Is (Systematic) Value Investing Dead?" (*Journal of Portfolio Management*, 2020/21; working paper dated 14 Mar 2020) concede that many systematic value approaches "have clearly struggled recently." They call the "value is dead" claim premature and rebut critiques such as the argument that price-to-book misses intangibles. — [AQR paper page](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/Is-Systematic-Value-Investing-Dead)
- Cliff Asness's companion piece (8 May 2020) argues that value is "not dead." He acknowledges that AQR has "a whole team of horses in this race," which is an explicit conflict of interest. — [Asness, "Is Systematic Value Investing Dead?" (AQR Perspective PDF)](https://www.aqr.com:443/-/media/AQR/Documents/Perspectives/Cliffs-Perspective-5620-Is-Systematic-Value-Investing-Dead.pdf?sc_lang=en)
- Robustness check: Asness tested other accounting measures and excluded winner-take-all companies, and found the value story unchanged. — [Institutional Investor, "Value Managers Fight Back"](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/2bsxa6cu6tic60cll6g3k/portfolio/value-managers-fight-back)

### Inferences
- "Buy quality at a reasonable price" (the Buffett/Munger principle) has academic support as a systematic factor premium (QMJ, BAB, value). That support undercuts the mystique: the edge can be largely replicated with rules, and Buffett's results depended on cheap, permanent leverage that individuals cannot copy.
- Factor premia are real but can disappear for a decade or more. The long value drawdown, roughly 2007 to late 2020, meant disciples of "value investing" endured years of underperformance. A principle that is right on average can still be unbearable to hold in practice.
- Bessembinder's skewness result cuts two ways. Concentrated portfolios such as Buffett's or Wood's are likely to miss the few big winners (so most concentrated portfolios lag), while the rare concentrated winners produce the stories that get told. That is survivorship bias built into the return distribution itself.
- Academic consensus is solid on two points: average net alpha is negative and roughly equals costs, and persistence is weak. The size of the skilled tail before fees is contested (Fama-French vs Kosowski et al. vs Harvey-Liu).

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the specific drawdown magnitude for the HML value factor, 2007–2020 (commonly cited as about a 50%+ cumulative drawdown of U.S. HML from its 2007 peak to 2020). This needs checking against Ken French's data library or AQR data before it is quoted.
- Bessembinder's later updates (for example, global stocks in 2019–2020, or a U.S. update to 2022/2023) were not retrieved.
- Carhart's abstract was not fetched this session. The summary above reflects the well-known published abstract.

## 3. The behaviour gap: investor returns vs fund returns

### Takeaway
Investors earn less than the funds they own because of poorly timed purchases and sales. Morningstar's dollar-weighted method puts the gap at about 1.2 percentage points a year over 10 years. It is larger for volatile, thematic and sector funds and near zero for target-date and allocation funds. Dalbar reports far larger gaps, but its method is widely criticized.

### Cited Findings
- **Morningstar Mind the Gap 2025 (10 years ended 31 Dec 2024):** the average dollar in U.S. mutual funds and ETFs earned about 7.0% a year, roughly 1.2 percentage points a year less than the funds' total returns. — [Morningstar Mind the Gap US 2025](https://www.morningstar.com/business/insights/research/mind-the-gap-2025); [WealthManagement.com, "Investors miss out on 12% of fund returns over decade"](https://wealthmanagement.com/investing-strategies/investors-miss-out-on-12-of-fund-returns-over-decade)
- By category: U.S. equity funds had a 0.4% annual gap and allocation funds 0.7%. Alternatives had 1.6% and sector equity 1.2%. Active-fund investors had a 1.6% gap versus 1.1% for index funds. The gap was about 2% in the most volatile funds versus 0.4% in the least volatile. Target-date funds showed almost no gap. — [Morningstar Mind the Gap 2025](https://www.morningstar.com/business/insights/research/mind-the-gap-2025) (via secondary summaries; the page did not render for direct reading)
- Prior edition (10 years to 2023): investors earned 6.3% a year against 7.3% for the funds, losing about 15% of total return. — [Morningstar Mind the Gap 2025](https://www.morningstar.com/business/insights/research/mind-the-gap-2025) (as cited by secondary coverage)
- Morningstar's Jeffrey Ptak links the gap to trading frequency ("the more investors trade, the less they earn") and to buying high and selling low. — [Ritholtz, "At The Money," Oct 2025](https://ritholtz.com/2025/10/atm-dont-underperform-your-own-investments/)
- **Dalbar QAIB 2025 (calendar 2024):** the average equity fund investor earned 16.54% against 25.02% for the S&P 500, an 848 bp gap and the second-largest in a decade. — [Dalbar 2025 QAIB press release](https://www.dalbar.com/Portals/dalbar/Cache/News/PressReleases/2025QAIBPressRelease.pdf)
- **Dalbar QAIB 2026 (calendar 2025):** investors earned 17.16% against 17.88% for the S&P 500, a 72 bp gap. — [Dalbar 2026 QAIB press release](https://www.dalbar.com/PressReleases/doc/2026QAIBReportPressRelease.pdf)

### Inferences
- Behavioural discipline (staying invested, avoiding performance-chasing) is one of the best-supported "principles." It is also a principle the hero investors preach, and their own funds' investors often fail to follow it (see the ARKK case in section 4).
- Gaps are largest exactly where star-manager narratives are strongest: volatile, thematic and active funds.
- The Dalbar figures swing wildly from year to year (848 bp, then 72 bp) and are methodologically contested. Morningstar's dollar-weighted fund-level method is the more defensible source for a guide.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch a published critique of Dalbar's methodology (for example by Wade Pfau or Michael Kitces). The contention that Dalbar overstates the gap is well known among practitioners but is not sourced here.
- Morningstar's 2026 Mind the Gap edition (10 years to end-2025), if published, was not found.

## 4. Cautionary 21st-century cases: star investors whose records reversed

### Takeaway
Many of the most celebrated "top investor" stories of 2000–2026 were followed by severe reversals. Paulson, Miller, Wood, Tiger Global and Archegos each show at least one failure mode: concentration, leverage, style drift or asset bloat after a hot streak, and investor flows that arrive at the peak. A single great call, as with Paulson in 2007 or Burry in 2008, has been a poor predictor of later results.

### Cited Findings
**John Paulson (Paulson & Co.)**
- Paulson became famous for his 2007 bet against subprime. In 2020 he announced he would return outside capital and convert Paulson & Co. into a family office, after a long period of falling assets and weaker returns. — [Gregory Zuckerman, "Hedge Fund Star John Paulson Calls It Quits," Aug 2020](https://www.gregoryzuckerman.com/2020/08/05/hedge-fund-star-john-paulson-calls-it-quits/); [Bloomberg via BloombergQuint](https://www.bloombergquint.com/markets/john-paulson-to-convert-hedge-fund-firm-into-a-family-office); [CNBC, 1 Jul 2020](https://www.cnbc.com/2020/07/01/john-paulson-is-reportedly-winding-down-his-hedge-funds-these-were-his-last-big-bets.html)
- His Advantage Plus fund reportedly lost about 51% in 2011, mainly on gold and bank stocks, and the gold fund lost about 63% between 2011 and 2014. Sino-Forest was a notable loss. These figures come from lower-quality aggregators and should be checked. — [Superinvestors profile (aggregator)](https://superinvestors-app.pages.dev/investors/john-paulson); [Wikipedia: Paulson & Co.](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Paulson_%26_Co.)
- The commonly cited figure of roughly $15B in 2007 profits for the firm was not verified this session (see Gaps).

**Bill Miller (Legg Mason Value Trust)**
- Miller beat the S&P 500 for 15 straight calendar years (1991–2005). The streak ended in 2006. — [Motley Fool, "Was Bill Miller Just Lucky?" 2008](https://www.fool.com/investing/mutual-funds/2008/07/21/was-bill-miller-just-lucky.aspx); [InvestmentNews](https://investmentnews.com/?p=68835)
- Value Trust fell about 55% in 2008 against -37% for the S&P 500, after big bets on financials during the subprime collapse. From 2009 to his exit in April 2012 it returned 15.3% a year against 16.5% for the S&P 500. — [Fool, "The Worst Mutual Funds of 2008"](https://www.fool.com/investing/mutual-funds/2009/01/06/the-worst-mutual-funds-of-2008.aspx); [InvestmentNews on Legg Mason](https://investmentnews.com/?p=68835)
- Later comeback: at Miller Value Partners (Opportunity Trust and a hedge fund) he had large Amazon and Bitcoin positions. Bloomberg reported his hedge fund "surges 46% riding Amazon, Bitcoin wave." He has said Bitcoin was about half his personal portfolio, at an average cost near $500. — [Bloomberg/BloombergQuint](https://www.bloombergquint.com/markets/bill-miller-s-hedge-fund-surges-46-riding-amazon-bitcoin-wave); [Validea, "Bill Miller Roars Back"](https://blog.validea.com/?p=27885); [Forbes Australia](https://www.forbes.com.au/covers/investing/legendary-investor-bill-miller-still-loves-bitcoin/)
- Statisticians have long debated whether the streak showed skill. Calendar-year streaks are an arbitrary measure, and among thousands of managers some long streak is likely. — [Motley Fool 2008](https://www.fool.com/investing/mutual-funds/2008/07/21/was-bill-miller-just-lucky.aspx)

**Cathie Wood / ARK Innovation ETF (ARKK)**
- Morningstar estimated that ARKK shareholders lost far more than the fund's reported returns because money poured in after the 2020 surge and left after the losses. In one period investors lost about 12% a year against the fund's reported -4.3%. A later update (to about May 2023) put the three-year dollar-weighted loss at around 28% a year, more than double the reported loss. Since-inception dollar-weighted returns were sharply negative even though time-weighted returns were positive. — [Morningstar, "ARKK: An Object Lesson in How Not to Invest"](https://www.morningstar.com/markets/arkk-an-object-lesson-how-not-invest); [Morningstar SG, "Why ARKK Investors Are Still Underwater"](https://sg.morningstar.com/sg/news/236131/why-arkk-investors-are-still-underwater.aspx)
- In Morningstar's ranking of "wealth destroyers," ARKK was third among funds over 10 years at -$7.1B, and ARK Invest topped the list of ETF issuers. — [Benzinga on Morningstar's Amy Arnott](https://benzinga.com/z/37018085); [Investing.com](https://investing.com/news/stock-market-news/ark-investments-tops-morningstar-list-of-wealthdestroying-etf-issuers-report-3293277)

**Tiger Global (Chase Coleman)**
- In 2022, Tiger Global's long/short hedge fund fell 56% and its long-only fund 67%, driven by tech and growth positions including JD.com and CrowdStrike. — [Institutional Investor, "Hedge Funds Lost More Than $200 Billion Last Year"](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/b8x4y67blyqdwz/hedge-funds-lost-more-than-200-billion-last-year); [Benzinga](https://benzinga.com/z/30376793)
- LCH Investments (Rick Sopher) estimated Tiger lost $18B in 2022, almost 9% of the $208.4B lost by the hedge fund industry that year. Another outlet reported total losses above $41B, which probably includes private and venture holdings, so the definition differs. — [Institutional Investor](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/b8x4y67blyqdwz/hedge-funds-lost-more-than-200-billion-last-year); [Hedge Fund Alpha, "Tiger Global's losses top $41bn in 2022"](https://hedgefundalpha.com/news/tiger-globals-losses-top-41bn-in-2022/)

**Archegos Capital (Bill Hwang), 2021**
- Archegos built concentrated, highly leveraged positions through total return swaps. When it collapsed in March 2021, the forced selling wiped out more than $100B in market value, and banks lost over $10B in total: Credit Suisse about $5.5B, Nomura about $2.85B. — [Al Jazeera, 20 Nov 2024](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2024/11/20/archegoss-bill-hwang-sentenced-to-18-years-in-prison-for-massive-us-fraud); [Wikipedia: Archegos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archegos_Capital_Management)
- Hwang was convicted in July 2024 on 10 counts, including securities fraud and market manipulation, and sentenced on 20 Nov 2024 to 18 years. He was appealing as of the reports found. — [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/archegos-bill-hwang-sentenced-18-years-prison-massive-us-fraud-rcna181134); [WealthManagement.com](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/ria-news/bill-hwang-sentenced-to-18-years-in-prison-for-archegos-crimes)

**Michael Burry (Scion Asset Management)**
- SEC records show Scion's registration as an investment adviser terminated effective 10 Nov 2025, after a final 13F on 3 Nov 2025. The public can therefore no longer track his holdings. Some analysts speculated about a family-office structure, but this is unconfirmed. Burry said his reported Palantir put position cost $9.2M, not the "$912M" notional figure. — [Bloomberg Government](https://news.bgov.com/private-equity/michael-burrys-scion-hedge-fund-deregistered-sec-filing-shows); [Investing.com](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/big-short-micheal-burry-deregisters-scion-asset-management-4353912); [Sherwood News](https://sherwood.news/markets/michael-burry-de-registers-his-hedge-fund-scion-asset-management-as-he-warns/)

### Inferences
- Common failure modes across the cases:
  - Concentration: Miller in financials in 2008, Tiger in growth tech, Archegos in a handful of names.
  - Leverage: Archegos, and the gold/bank losses inside leveraged Paulson vehicles.
  - Asset growth after a hot streak, so most investor dollars arrive near the peak (ARKK, Tiger).
  - Style or thesis drift: Paulson moving from credit shorts to a gold macro bet.
- One spectacular call (Paulson 2007, Burry 2008) is a poor basis for imitation. Both later had mixed or opaque records, and both eventually stopped reporting publicly.
- Miller's case shows that even a 15-year streak does not establish persistent skill, and that later recoveries were driven by a few concentrated bets (Amazon, Bitcoin), consistent with Bessembinder's skewness.

### Gaps
- Paulson's 2007 profit (often cited as about $15B for the firm and about $3.7–4B personally) and the $9.4B client-loss figure attributed to Bloomberg were not verified from primary text.
- ARKK's calendar-year returns (about +150% in 2020, about -67% in 2022) are widely reported, but I did not fetch a primary source for them. The Morningstar pages returned HTTP 403 and figures come from search excerpts.
- Burry's post-2008 performance is largely unverifiable because Scion's returns were not public. I found no reliable source quantifying his "mixed" record.
- Miller's exact retirement date and verified Opportunity Trust annual returns were not found from primary sources. The claim of about +120% in 2021 is unverified.

## 5. Buffett's 10-year bet and Bogle's case for indexing

### Takeaway
Buffett's own bet supports the low-cost index principle he recommends to non-professionals, rather than imitating his stock-picking. Over 2008–2017, an S&P 500 index fund returned about 7.1% a year, while Protégé Partners' basket of hedge funds of funds returned about 2.2% a year (about 2.1% for the full term per WSJ/BI).

### Cited Findings
- The Vanguard 500 Index Fund Admiral Shares returned 7.1% compounded annually over the bet period, against about 2.2% a year for the average of Protégé Partners' five funds-of-hedge-funds (WSJ, via CNBC). — [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/id/104926483?view=story); [Kiplinger, "Warren Buffett: Why Index Funds Trump Hedge Funds"](https://www.kiplinger.com/article/investing/T030-C008-S001-warren-buffett-why-index-funds-trump-hedge-funds.html)
- Sources disagree slightly. Kiplinger ties 2.2% to the period through end-2016, while Business Insider (citing WSJ) gives about 2.1% for the full term through end-2017. Cumulatively the index fund gained about 85.4% and the hedge fund basket about 22%. — [Business Insider India](https://www.businessinsider.in/warren-buffett-has-won-his-1-million-bet-against-the-hedge-fund-industry/articleshow/62342782.cms); [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2015/02/03/berkshires-buffett-adds-to-his-lead-in-1-million-bet-with-hedge-fund)
- Girls Inc. of Omaha received $2,222,279 because the escrow had been moved into Berkshire B shares. — [Business Insider India](https://www.businessinsider.in/warren-buffett-has-won-his-1-million-bet-against-the-hedge-fund-industry/articleshow/62342782.cms)

### Inferences
- The "learn from Buffett" lesson that the evidence supports best is Buffett's own advice for most people: buy a low-cost S&P 500 index fund. The bet was largely a test of fees. Funds-of-funds layered two sets of fees on hedge fund returns.
- Bogle's "cost matters hypothesis" in *The Little Book of Common Sense Investing* (2007; updated 2017) holds that investors as a group earn the market return minus costs, so the average active investor must trail a low-cost index. This is consistent with Fama-French's finding that aggregate net alpha is about minus expenses, and with SPIVA. (The book was not fetched this session; this is a standard summary of its central argument.)

### Gaps
- The book itself was not fetched, so there are no direct page-cited quotations from Bogle.
- The identities of Protégé's five funds were never disclosed, so their exact strategies cannot be analyzed.

## 6. Which "top investor" principles survive scrutiny?

### Takeaway
Principles backed by strong evidence are low costs, broad diversification for non-professionals, a long horizon with behavioural discipline, and avoiding leverage and ruin. "Buy quality and value at reasonable prices" is supported as a factor premium but with long, painful droughts. Principles that are mainly survivorship-biased stories include concentrated conviction bets, copying a star's recent record, and treating a single famous call as proof of repeatable skill.

### Cited Findings (evidence map; each row links to the evidence above)
- **Low costs: strongly supported.** Net alpha of active funds is about minus fees ([Fama & French 2010](https://mba.tuck.dartmouth.edu/bespeneckbo/default/AFA611-Eckbo%20web%20site/AFA611-S8C-FamaFrench-LuckvSkill-JF10.pdf)). Expenses explain much of the persistence ([Carhart 1997](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1997.tb03808.x)). The index fund beat the hedge fund basket ([Buffett bet, CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/id/104926483?view=story)).
- **Diversification and indexing for non-professionals: strongly supported.** 90–95% of U.S. active funds lagged over 10–20 years ([SPIVA YE2025](https://www.ariesadvisors.com/files/spiva-us-year-end-2025.pdf)). Wealth creation is concentrated in about 4% of stocks, so missing them is costly ([Bessembinder 2018](https://wpcarey.asu.edu/sites/default/files/2021-10/do-stocks-outperform-treasury-bills.pdf)).
- **Behavioural discipline and a long horizon: strongly supported.** Investors trail their own funds by about 1.2 points a year, more in volatile funds ([Morningstar Mind the Gap 2025](https://www.morningstar.com/business/insights/research/mind-the-gap-2025)). ARKK investors lost far more than the fund itself ([Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/markets/arkk-an-object-lesson-how-not-invest)).
- **Avoid leverage and ruin: supported by case evidence.** Archegos and the collapses of concentrated, leveraged books ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2024/11/20/archegoss-bill-hwang-sentenced-to-18-years-in-prison-for-massive-us-fraud)). One caveat: Buffett used leverage of about 1.6–1.7x, but it was cheap, stable insurance float, not margin or swaps ([Buffett's Alpha](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3197185)).
- **Quality and value at reasonable prices: supported as systematic factors, contested on timing.** Buffett's alpha is explained by BAB and QMJ ([Buffett's Alpha](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3197185)). Value had a long drought that even its proponents acknowledge ([AQR 2020](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/Is-Systematic-Value-Investing-Dead)).
- **Picking proven star managers or copying a hot record: not supported.** Almost no top-quartile funds stay there ([SPIVA Persistence YE2025](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/documents/spiva/persistence-scorecard-year-end-2025.pdf)). See also Miller, Paulson, ARKK and Tiger above.

### Inferences
- The "skill" in great investors that survives scrutiny is mostly replicable process: factor exposures, patience, low turnover, cheap funding. It is not a secret insight to copy from 13F filings. The behaviours they model (patience, avoiding ruin, discipline) generalize better than their stock picks.
- A guide should present each investor's principles with a warning: we study these people because they survived and won. For every Buffett there are many concentrated value investors who closed, and SPIVA shows two-thirds of funds disappear within 20 years.
- Evidence also cuts against pure indexing purism in one respect. A small skilled tail may exist before fees (contested in academia), but the evidence suggests it is rarely identifiable in advance and rarely captured by investors after fees.

### Gaps
- There is no single meta-study directly testing "principles of famous investors." This mapping is a synthesis of separate bodies of evidence.
- Evidence on 13F copycat or "cloning" strategies (whether mirroring famous investors' disclosed holdings earns excess returns) was not researched this session and would be a useful addition.
